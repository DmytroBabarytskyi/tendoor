Public e-procurement systems publish millions of purchased items with unit prices, yet price screening still relies on manual comparison or on procedure-level red flags that ignore what was bought and at what price. We present a two-stage method for detecting anomalous unit prices of individual procured items and explaining each decision. The first stage makes item records comparable - converting per-package prices to prices per kilogram or litre, grouping items by classification code and unit, and splitting groups by the semantics of their descriptions - and screens each group with robust statistical and density-based rules. The second stage, a monotone gradient-boosting ranking model, re-scores the screening signals by how likely an annotator is to confirm the item; decisions are explained with Shapley values. We evaluate on 16,103 food items from the Ukrainian Prozorro system with a stratified, design-weighted sample of 400 items. The labels were produced by a large language model under a written protocol and checked by a non-expert human on 80 items (kappa = 0.64); they are not verdicts of professional auditors. A sighted annotation round confirmed the earlier system's flags more often than a blind round, a shift consistent with anchoring. On blind labels, the ranking model raises the design-weighted average precision from 0.49 for the first system version and 0.51 for the best statistical rule to 0.81 (95% bootstrap interval of the gain over the rule: 0.10-0.44); with the human corrections the gain over the rule shrinks to 0.09 and is no longer significant (-0.09 to 0.30). On injected errors, flagging gross extremes independently of the ranking model is needed to keep the recall of unit-scale errors. Explanations from 24 Monte Carlo permutations are within about one point of a high-precision reference on a 0-100 risk scale. It is implemented in an open-source information system.
