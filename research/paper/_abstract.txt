Public e-procurement systems publish millions of purchased items with unit prices, yet audit practice screens prices manually or relies on procedure-level red flags that ignore what was bought and at what price. We present a two-stage method for detecting anomalous unit prices of individual procured items and explaining each decision. The first stage makes item records comparable - converting per-package prices to prices per kilogram or litre, grouping items by classification code and unit, and splitting groups by the semantics of their descriptions - and screens each group with robust statistical and density-based rules. The second stage, a monotone gradient-boosting calibrator trained on review verdicts, turns screening signals into the probability that a reviewer would confirm the anomaly; decisions are explained with Shapley values. We evaluate on 16,103 food items from the Ukrainian Prozorro system with a stratified, design-weighted annotation of 400 items. An annotator who sees the model's decision confirms its flags more often; we therefore re-annotate blind (agreement kappa = 0.82) and verify a subsample by a human (kappa = 0.64). On blind labels, calibration raises average precision from 0.49 to 0.81 (95% CI of the gain 0.17-0.43), outperforming statistical rules and unsupervised detectors; the comparability layer matters more than the choice of detector. On injected errors the calibrator transfers to a held-out category, and flagging gross extremes regardless of the calibrator restores recall of unit-scale errors. Explanations from 24 Monte Carlo permutations are within one risk point of a reference and faithful in a deletion test. The method is implemented in an open-source information system.
